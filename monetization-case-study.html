Research.

This case study is a research-led exploration of how streaming platforms can be clearer about what a subscription actually covers, using Amazon Prime Video as the test case. Working alongside Ashley Sinclair and Armando Troisi, I helped lead the research, design three independent interface concepts, and test each one with real users.

Each concept approached the same goal — making included and paid content easier to tell apart — through a different usability lens. We measured all three against user feedback and Prime Video's own business goals before recommending a direction.

01  ·  Problem

An impressive catalogue, wrapped in confusion.

Spending time investigating the platform firsthand, we found that Amazon Prime Video offers an impressive variety of shows and movies — but it's genuinely unclear what's included in the subscription price, and what isn't.

Content Fragmentation

A mix of free-with-Prime, rental, and purchase titles sit side by side in the same rows, making it hard to tell what's included without clicking in — frustrating, and sometimes costly.

Intrusive Ads & Upselling

Ads and prompts to buy or rent content interrupt viewing, blurring the line between what the subscription already covers and what costs extra.

Poor Search & Discovery

Search results often lack clarity, and the overall browsing flow feels disorganized next to competitors like Netflix.

The kind of mid-browse upsell prompt users described as confusing and easy to stop trusting.

04  ·  Business Goals

Clarity has to survive contact with the business model.

Prime Video's own merchandising logic points the other way on some of these patterns — any fix we proposed had to hold up against goals the platform is already optimising for.

01

Mixed Content

Users tend to spend more when paid and free content are shown side by side in the same row.

02

Okay with Ugly

Denser, option-heavy pages can increase conversion for certain customer segments — clarity and conversion don't always pull in the same direction.

03

One-Way Doors

Removing back buttons at monetization decision points keeps users from second-guessing their way out of a purchase.

How might we improve clarity among Prime-included, subscription, and pay-per-title content — while still aligning with Prime Video's business goals?
05  ·  Design Proposal

Three concepts, three usability lenses.

We translated the research and business goals into three independent interface concepts — each solving the same transparency problem through a different heuristic. We prototyped and tested all three with real users before recommending a direction.

Concept 1  ·  Blackout

Make the unavailable look unavailable.

Visibility of System Status

Every row on Prime Video's homepage mixes titles you already have with titles you'd need another subscription to watch — with no visual difference between the two until you tap in. We dimmed unsubscribed cards with a scrim to signal they're currently unavailable, then added a centered service badge to telegraph exactly which paywall is waiting behind the tap.

Before
After
Testing Results — 90% / 75% / 50% Opacity

We tested the scrim at three opacity levels with five users, scoring each on a 1–5 scale across four metrics.

Metric 90% Opacity 75% Opacity 50% Opacity
Clarity of Prime membership inclusion 4 4.33 4.67
Understanding show identity (pre-hover) 3 3.67 4.67
Did you expect the paywall? 4.67 4.67 4.33
Sense of control in purchase decision 5 5 5
Why 50% Opacity

50% won outright on clarity and show identity, and tied every condition on sense of control — with only a slightly lower score on whether users expected the paywall at all. We chose it as the strongest overall balance.

Concept 2  ·  Card Banner

Label the paywall instead of hiding it.

Visibility of System Status

Where Blackout subtracts information from unsubscribed cards, Card Banner adds it — a consistent coloured banner under every poster naming which service a title actually belongs to, standardised in colour, icon placement, and position across the whole grid.

Concept 3  ·  Toggle

Surface the filter nobody noticed.

User Control and Freedom

Prime Video's interface already lets viewers filter between Prime-included and subscription content — but none of the people we interviewed knew the controls existed. We redesigned it as a single, brightly coloured toggle fixed in the main navigation.

Testing Results

06  ·  Outcomes & Next Steps

One concept validated, two with a clear next move.

We tested all three concepts against an interactive Figma prototype ↗.

Blackout

Validated

Scored highest across the usability metrics we tracked. Next: test it against Prime Video's actual conversion and engagement data, not just usability scores.

Card Banner

Inconclusive

Tested just as clearly as Blackout, but users preferred a subtractive approach on principle. Next: redesign and retest the visual language before ruling it out.

Toggle

Needs Iteration

3 of 4 users missed it entirely on first look. Next: iterate on placement and visual weight, then test again.

07  ·  Reflection

What I'd do differently.

Test Discoverability Earlier

3 of 4 users missed the Toggle in testing. A quick round of guerrilla testing on the icon alone, before building the full flow around it, would have caught that earlier and cheaper.

Additive Isn't Always the Answer

Card Banner tested just as clearly as Blackout on paper, but informal feedback favoured Blackout's subtractive approach — a reminder that clarity sometimes comes from removing signal, not adding more of it.

Test Against the Business, Not Just the User

We validated Blackout with users but haven't yet tested it against Prime Video's actual conversion goals — the gap we flagged for ourselves going into the next round.
